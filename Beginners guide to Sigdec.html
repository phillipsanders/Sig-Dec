Who tells the AI what we decided?

    AI tools now build many of our screens. They follow the rules they are given and guess at the rest. This tutorial shows a small practice, Signed Decisions, for getting a team’s product decisions to the machine with a name, a rank and an end date attached.

    DEC-0004MUST
    Confirm before any action that removes data or moves money.
    Because in shared accounts the harm falls on the person who didn’t act.
    Product and Legalreview Oct 2027

    That slip is the whole idea. Nine short steps follow, each with something to try. Examples come from Harbor, a fictional bank used in the experiment behind this work.

    Step 1

    A value is not a meaning

    A design token stores a value, such as a colour. Two reds can sit side by side in the file with nothing to say which one means danger. An AI asked for a Delete button has to guess.

    Try it: describe the tokens

    color.brand.accent
    no description
    color.feedback.danger
    no description
    motion.duration.fast
    150 ms

    The AI builds:

    Remove payee “L. Martin”?
    Delete
    It picked the brand red. Nothing told it otherwise.

    An illustration of Romina Kavcic’s 2026 example. In the Harbor experiment, a one-line description was enough for this kind of case: the danger colour was right in 4 or 5 of 5 runs in every setup.

    So the first lesson is modest. For anything a token can carry, describe the token. Signed Decisions is for what a token cannot carry.

    Step 2

    Three homes, one fact in each

    Not everything is a decision. Following Cassie Groos’s principle of “one origin per fact”, each piece of knowledge gets exactly one home.

    HomeHoldsExample
    TokenA value and what it is forHow fast a panel slides in
    CRD (Component Requirements Document)What one component must do, and whyA button has one icon, never two
    Signed decisionA rule that crosses components and has consequencesAnything that moves money confirms first

    Try it: sort these six

    The CRD, or Component Requirements Document, is Cassie Groos’s work: one plain-language file per component, co-authored by product managers, designers and developers and maintained by the AI agents that work from it. Signed Decisions sits beside it and does not replace it.

    Step 3

    What a signed decision contains

    One record, two depths. The top is short and goes to the AI. The evidence underneath stays with the team unless someone asks for it.

    Not: an undo toast. Do: a dialog that names what will be lost.

    Most of the time the short line is enough. When the AI cannot tell whether a decision applies to the case in front of it, it first reads the justification. If that does not settle it, it asks the person who launched it to make the call, with one line on why, and finishes the work. Afterwards it logs the question and the call, marks the work, and flags it to the decision’s signers, who confirm, reword or overrule it.
    

    



    
  
    Step 4
    
  
    Record it now, justify it later
    
  
    Recording a decision and justifying it are two separate acts. The decision is recorded the moment it is made, in about ten seconds, with a one-line reason. The fuller justification, meaning the evidence behind it, can be added later and only if it is needed.
    
  
    Nobody is asked to compose a perfect sentence either. An assistant asks three questions, and rewrites the answer in Plain English or in a controlled technical English adjusted for the industry, so every decision in the folder reads the same way and uses the same words for the same things.

    Step 5
    
  
    The brief gets checked before anything is built
    
  
    When someone writes a brief or a prompt, the same assistant compares it with the standing decisions. If the brief contradicts one, it asks. If the brief merely touches one, it pins that decision’s ID to the brief so the builder receives it.

    Step 7
    
  
    A second look at the finished flow
    
  
    Designs change after the brief. Before a flow is handed to developers, the assistant scans it against the decisions and leaves short notes. It advises and asks. It does not block. Hard enforcement comes later, from automated checks.

    Designers keep working on the canvas throughout. Decisions limit what a screen may do; they never dictate how it looks.
    

    



    
  
    Step 8
    
  
    Decisions are meant to leave
    
  
    A decision that holds up moves into a more permanent home: a component’s CRD, a token, or an automated check. One that stops being true expires. The set should stay small enough to read in a sitting.

    Step 9
    
  
    The folder can seed itself
    
  
    An empty folder is the hardest moment for any new practice. On adoption, the assistant can read what the team has already produced, such as the last twenty briefs, the design flows, the code that shipped and the meeting notes, and draft the first twenty decisions from them. Nothing it drafts is live until the function that owns the rule signs it.

    The same reading shows where decisions are missing or contradict each other, for example no record of Legal being consulted, or two teams that have drifted apart without anyone choosing. A gap means no record was found, which is not proof that nothing happened. Findings are reported by role and team, never by person, and only shared work documents are read.

    Four layers
    
    
    
    
    
  
    

  
    What it is
    
    Signed Decisions is a small folder of short product decisions, with an assistant skill and a small tool that read them. Each decision has a rank, a scope, a one-line reason, the functions that signed it and a review date. The skill is used when a decision is captured, when a brief is written, before an AI builds, and when a finished flow or a published design file is checked.
    
    
    It is built to sit beside Cassie Groos’s CRDs (Component Requirements Documents). A CRD holds what one component must do. Signed decisions hold the rules that belong to no single component, which a CRD can cite.
    
  
    

  
    The record format (proposed)

    ---
id: DEC-0004
status: accepted
rank: MUST
scope: [action:delete, action:transfer, state:destructive]
decision-makers: [product, legal]
review: 2027-10
justification: history/destructive-actions.md
verify: confirm-before-destructive
---
Any action that removes data or moves money MUST confirm first.
Because in shared accounts the harm falls on the person who did not act.
Not: immediate action with an undo toast.
Do: a dialog that names what will be lost.
    
    
    One short file per decision. The justification is not copied into it: the file links to the history entry that explains the decision. A sample folder in this format, and a tool that checks it, are in the Sig-Dec repository on GitHub (phillipsanders/Sig-Dec).
    
  
    

  
    How it reaches the machine
    
      
      
    • At adoption. The assistant reads recent briefs, flows, code and meeting notes, drafts the first decisions and reports gaps. Every draft waits for a signature.
      • 
      
    • At capture. A three-question interview records the decision at once, with a one-line reason, and sets the rank. The assistant rewrites the wording in Plain English or a controlled technical English adjusted for the industry. The function that owns the rule signs by approving it. The justification is a separate, later step.
      • 
      
    • At the brief. The skill checks the draft, asks about contradictions, and pins the IDs of decisions the work touches.
      • 
      
    • At build. The AI receives only the pinned decisions, top part only, strictest first, five at most. The file of standing instructions the AI always reads is generated from the folder, never written by hand: it holds a pointer to the decisions and the few that apply to every task.
      • 
      
    • When in doubt. If the AI cannot tell whether a decision applies, it reads that decision’s justification. If it is still unsure, it asks the person who launched it to make the call, finishes the work, then logs the call and flags it to the decision’s signers.
      • 
      
    • At review. The skill scans the finished flow and annotates deviations. A decision can also name a check that runs each time the design file is published; a failure goes to its signers.
      • 
      
    • Over time. Stable decisions are promoted into a CRD statement, a token or an automated check.
      • 
    
    
  
    

  
    How four known problems are handled
    
    
      
    ProblemAnswer in the design
    
      
    A skill in a code repository only reaches people who use developer toolsOne folder, several doors. The same check is offered where briefs are written: a ticket tool, a document, a design tool. Each door is a thin window onto an assistant that can see the folder, never a separate form
    
      
    Matching a brief to decisions by meaning can miss or raise false alarmsExact scope tags first. Meaning-based matches are labelled “possible” and need a person’s confirmation. The pinned list is visible on the brief, and capped at five
    
      
    An AI scanning a flow is an unreliable gatekeeperThe scan only advises. Blocking is done by ordinary automated checks, and only for decisions that have been promoted into one
    
      
    Everything gets filed as a decisionA sorting rule: a value is a token; one component’s behaviour belongs in its CRD; a decision is cross-cutting, has consequences, and someone could plausibly propose the opposite
    
    
    
    
    Three earlier problems are also addressed. A decision without a reason is not accepted, though the reason is one line. When two decisions disagree, the stricter rank wins; at the same rank, the one that names the other as overridden, then the narrower scope, then the author of the brief. Two MUSTs that conflict always go to a person, with both signers named. And usage figures are shown privately to the writer and by role to everyone else, under the rule “ask me why, never blame me when”.
    
  
    

  
    What is shown, and what is not
    
    
      
    Shown in one experimentNot yet shown
    
      
    Ranked, scoped decisions delivered at build time resolved a collision: 9 of 10 runs visibly, 10 of 10 counting one hidden dialog, against 0 of 20 for every other setupThat people will write decisions and keep writing them
    
      
    A plain rules file noticed the collision in 5 of 5 runs and still chose the unsafe optionThe brief check, the finished-flow scan, and the AI reading a justification or asking the designer when unsure
    
      
    Out-of-scope decisions were opened and not applied, in 10 of 10 runsWhether the AI needs the reason or only the rank
    
      
    Described tokens alone handled the simple colour caseAny saving in cost or time, and any gain in consistency: the experiment found none
    
      
    That record-now, justify-later capture keeps people writing, and that corrected wording improves results
    
      
    That seeding from past work produces decisions people are willing to sign
    
    
    
    
    One model, one banking flow, five runs per setup, automated checks. No person scored the results. Treat it as a strong signal on one case. The decisions in the experiment used the strictness words working, binding and mandatory; MUST, SHOULD and MAY came afterwards and have not yet been tested.
